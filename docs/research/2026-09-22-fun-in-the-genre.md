# What makes political and management games fun, and what kills it

Research notes for United States of Jev. Every row is a claim from a named source.

## 1. Findings

| Source | Principle | How it lands in United States of Jev |
|---|---|---|
| Sid Meier, GDC 2012 "Interesting Decisions" ([gamedeveloper.com](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/gdc-2012-sid-meier-on-how-to-see-games-as-sets-of-interesting-decisions)) | A decision is interesting only with a tradeoff, situational effect, style expression and persistent impact; it is dead if there is a dominant answer or a hidden consequence. | Every bill must cost one resource to buy another. Show the predicted swing on all five before the player commits. |
| Cliff Harris, "Democracy 4's overcomplexity is by design" ([positech.co.uk](https://www.positech.co.uk/cliffsblog/2023/02/19/democracy-4s-overcomplexity-is-by-design/)) | Harris keeps the model deliberately overwhelming and accepts that he "definitely lose[s] players, and generate[s] refunds among some, because they find the game 'not fun' and 'over-complex'." | Five resources, not twenty-five, is a deliberate split from Democracy. Keep it. Complexity belongs in what the legislators do, not in the dial count. |
| Democracy 4 negative reviews, Steam and Metacritic ([metacritic.com](https://www.metacritic.com/game/democracy-4/user-reviews/)) | Once the model is learned the game collapses into "moving sliders to keep other graphs happy"; reviewers report boredom within a few hours. | The escalation stack answers this. Test it: if a player wins term 3 without reading a bill, the escalations are too weak. |
| Democracy 4 forum thread on negative reviews ([steamcommunity.com](https://steamcommunity.com/app/1410710/discussions/0/3172198151266688767/)) | The loudest complaint is not difficulty, it is that one ideological path is mechanically favoured and the other is punished by the model. | Jev is a judge, not a rulebook, so its priors are invisible and untestable. Run a bias audit: same bill text, opposite ideological framing, measure the vote delta. Publish the number to yourself before launch. |
| François Alliot, Reigns deep dive ([gamedeveloper.com](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/game-design-deep-dive-creating-an-adaptive-narrative-in-i-reigns-i-)) | Playtests gave 10 minutes of interest; weighting every swipe against four power meters raised it to about 2 hours. Meaning came from the meters, not the writing. | The five failure lines are the whole game. Every free-text law must visibly move at least one meter, and the player must see which. Narration without a meter move is decoration. |
| Reigns card system, same source | Cards come from a weighted bag filtered by world state, so players cannot tell authored content from generated content. | The Director should weight crisis cards by state rather than fire on a timer, so a crisis reads as a consequence of the player's own play. |
| Nerial GDC talk, "The Casual (but Regal) Swipe" ([gdcvault.com](https://www.gdcvault.com/play/1028689/The-Casual-\(but-Regal\)-Swipe)) | The team fixed the input gesture first and explored content inside that constraint. | Free text is the opposite of a fixed gesture. Fix the frame instead: one bill per turn, one post per turn, same slots every time. |
| Torpor Games interview on Suzerain ([int-magazine.com](https://int-magazine.com/interview/ata-sergey-nowak-of-suzerain/)) | No single act ruins the country; consequences are the sum of a string of choices, and the information reaching the player is deliberately biased. | Avoid instant-death bills. A failure line crossed at turn 14 should trace to turns 3, 7 and 11. Let Luna's headlines be partisan and sometimes wrong. |
| Suzerain player criticism ([steamcommunity.com](https://steamcommunity.com/app/1207650/discussions/0/4358995119363747661)) | The main frustration is opacity: players cannot weigh cost against benefit because the decision does not say what it trades. | Free text plus a hidden judge is maximum opacity. A pre-vote read on the chamber is the highest-value screen in the game. |
| Henrik Fåhraeus, "Emergent Stories in Crusader Kings II", GDC 2014 ([media.gdcvault.com](https://media.gdcvault.com/GDC2014/Presentations/Fahraeus_Henrik_Emergent_Stories_in.pdf)) | The stories come from characters with traits, ambitions and opinions of each other, not from the strategy layer. | Give the 60 to 100 legislators names, two traits and a grudge. A vote is only a story when the player can say "Hargrove voted no again." |
| David Dunham, King of Dragon Pass ([a-sharp.com](https://www.a-sharp.com/kodp/overview.html), [failbettergames.com](https://www.failbettergames.com/news/anything-nice-king-of-dragon-pass-david-dunham)) | Advisors disagree in cheap hover text; the low commitment of reading it is why players attach to characters who only speak in short bubbles. | Before each bill, show two or three legislator lines that disagree. Cheap for Luna, and it turns the chamber into a cast. |
| Frostpunk, Book of Laws analysis ([gamedeveloper.com](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/frostpunk-an-analysis-of-emotional-narrative-engagement)) | Irreversibility creates guilt: once a law is picked its alternative is gone. The analyst adds that Frostpunk's precise weather forecast reduces tension. | Make some laws permanent for the term. For crises, give a direction and a rough window, not an exact turn and number. |
| Lucas Pope, "Designing the bleak genius of Papers, Please" ([gamedeveloper.com](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/designing-the-bleak-genius-of-i-papers-please-i-)) | Moral weight comes from an unbalanced system where the path of least resistance is only visible in hindsight, not from scripted moral prompts. | Never ask the player "was that right?". Let the safe bill be genuinely safe, and let the end-of-term report show what it cost. |
| Raph Koster, A Theory of Fun ([emshort.blog](https://emshort.blog/how-to-play/writing-if/books-and-other-resources/a-theory-of-fun/)) | Fun is the brain consuming patterns; once a pattern is chunked it becomes autopilot and the game becomes boring. | The two escalations per extra term must change the pattern, not scale the numbers. A tougher threshold is arithmetic. A chamber that now leaks your drafts is a new pattern. |
| Ryan, Rigby and Przybylski, "The Motivational Pull of Video Games" ([selfdeterminationtheory.org](https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/SDT/documents/2006_RyanRigbyPrzybylski_MandE.pdf)) | Perceived autonomy and competence predict enjoyment and further play, and both depend on controls feeling intuitive. | Free text is high autonomy and low competence: players cannot tell if their drafting is improving. Add a per-bill margin history across the term. |
| Hunicke, LeBlanc and Zubek, MDA ([users.cs.northwestern.edu](https://users.cs.northwestern.edu/~hunicke/MDA.pdf)) | Designers build mechanics upward and hope for feeling; players meet the feeling first and infer the rules later. | Players will reverse-engineer Jev. Whatever the model actually weighs will become the metagame within a week. Decide what you want that metagame to be. |
| Jonasson and Purho, "Juice it or lose it" ([youtube.com](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Fy0aCDmgnxg)) | Identical mechanics feel flat or alive depending entirely on feedback layers: motion, sound, particles, timing. | The 0.5 second judgement is a gift. Roll the vote count up, flip legislator portraits one by one, hold the result one beat. Do not print the number instantly. |
| Josh Wardle on Wordle, and design analyses ([en.wikipedia.org](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wordle), [ai.glossika.com](https://ai.glossika.com/blog/how-wordle-got-you-hooked)) | One puzzle per day, the same for everyone, creates scarcity, a shared conversation and a ritual, and caps the session near three minutes. | A daily seeded term plus a shareable result grid is a larger retention lever than any balance change. Keep practice runs separate. |
| Balatro and Slay the Spire player discussion ([resetera.com](https://www.resetera.com/threads/why-is-balatro-so-addicting.1233393/page-2)) | Compulsion comes from short runs, visible exponential growth and loud per-action feedback, plus unlock requirements that outlast a single run. | 20 turns is the right length only if a run can be abandoned early and restarted in one click. Make approval and party mood visibly compound, not drift linearly. |
| Subset Games on Into the Breach ([gamedeveloper.com](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/how-subset-games-made-the-jump-from-i-ftl-i-to-i-into-the-breach-i-)) | They cut randomness so that a loss is legible as a player mistake rather than bad luck. | If Jev is stochastic, a lost vote reads as noise. Either make identical input give identical output within a run, or show the seed and the margin so the player can see how close it was. |
| LLM roleplay complaint survey ([bex.co](https://bex.co/blog/2025/04/17/negative-feedback-on-llm-powered-storytelling-and-roleplay-apps)) | Recurring complaints are memory loss, repetition, incoherence and latency; AI Dungeon had "multi-second or even minute-long waits for each action". | Luna must never block the turn. Verdict first, headline streamed after. Template the repeated beats. |
| FicMachine devlog on AI Dungeon ([itch.io](https://itch.io/devlog/1463303/why-ficmachine.amp)) | AI Dungeon fails because it is passive: it does not build toward anything, so scenes "stretched on and on without any sense of momentum" and the player ends up waiting. | This is exactly what the Director exists to prevent. Give the Director a required escalation: if no resource has moved more than a set amount in three turns, it must fire a crisis. |

## 2. The five things players say make them quit

1. **The model is solved.** Democracy 4 reviewers describe the game collapsing into sliders and graphs once the causal web is learned, with boredom arriving in the first few hours. Mastery arrives and nothing replaces it.
2. **The choice is opaque.** Suzerain players report they cannot weigh cost against benefit, so decisions feel arbitrary. Sid Meier names hidden consequences as the thing that ruins a decision.
3. **The choice was fake.** Players stop when they find that different inputs produce the same outcome. This is the standing charge against AI Dungeon: the system absorbs anything and nothing pushes back.
4. **It is biased against my play.** The Democracy 4 threads are dominated by players who believe one ideology is mechanically favoured and quit rather than play the designer's politics.
5. **It repeats itself.** LLM storytelling apps are criticised for cyclical output and forgotten context, and Frostpunk analysis notes that fully telegraphed pressure drains tension. Repetition kills faster than difficulty does.

## 3. What an LLM judge and narrator uniquely enable

Three things no game on this list can do.

**Unbounded input with bounded output.** Democracy 4 gives a fixed policy list; Reigns gives two swipes. Jev accepts any law the player can phrase and still returns a vote count. That moves the core verb from selection to authorship. Autonomy is the strongest predictor of enjoyment in the self-determination research, and free text is the highest autonomy input available.

**A chamber that reads intent, not keywords.** Crusader Kings needs hand-authored modifiers for every interaction. A judge model infers how a named legislator with two traits reacts to a bill nobody wrote in advance, so 60 to 100 distinct reactions cost the same as one.

**Narration that cites the player's own words.** Luna can quote the player's post back through a hostile columnist. King of Dragon Pass built strong attachment from short advisor bubbles alone, and quoting the player cannot be pre-written.

Two failure modes come with it.

**The rubber wall.** The judge always returns a plausible verdict, so every law "works" in the sense that something happens. When nothing can be refused on principle, the world stops feeling solid. The fix is hard state the model cannot override: an empty treasury fails the bill whatever the model thinks, and the model only sets margins inside limits the code enforces.

**Invisible priors that become the metagame.** MDA says players infer rules from feel. Whatever Jev rewards, players will find it and write to it, and if that is a political lean, the Democracy 4 reception is the preview. A judge cannot be balance-patched the way a spreadsheet can.

Latency is a constraint rather than a failure mode. A 0.5 second verdict sits inside what people accept for a turn; narration that waits on a second model does not, so it must stream after the verdict.

## 4. Open questions for the owner

1. **Is the daily seeded term in v1 or not?** Wordle's scarcity effect is the largest retention lever found here, and it changes save format, sharing and leaderboard design. Decide before the schema hardens.
2. **What is the metagame you want?** Players will optimise whatever Jev rewards. Rhetorical skill, policy realism and coalition arithmetic are three different games. Pick one as the primary signal and tune the prompt toward it.
3. **How much does the player see before committing?** A pre-vote estimate raises competence and cuts the opacity complaint, but it also removes surprise and makes the judge's priors visible and gameable. There is a real tradeoff here.
4. **Are the two escalations per term new patterns or harder numbers?** Koster's chunking argument says only new patterns extend the life of the loop.
5. **Is Jev deterministic within a run?** This decides whether a lost vote reads as a mistake or as noise, and therefore whether losing is instructive.
6. **What can the model never overrule?** Name the hard invariants now. That list is the difference between a world and a rubber wall.
